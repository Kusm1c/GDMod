01 The impossible problem

One button. One hit and you're dead.

In Geometry Dash your icon flies forward at constant speed on its own. You get exactly one control — a click — and its meaning changes with the vehicle: the cube jumps, the ship flies up, the wave darts diagonally. Touch a single spike and the run ends instantly.

Hard levels are built out of gaps you can only clear with frame-perfect clicks — press one frame too early or too late and you die. A demon level is thousands of those in a row. That's what makes it a beautiful puzzle for a computer: it's a long chain of split-second, life-or-death decisions with a simple rule set.

⌗ Under the hood — why brute force is hopeless Every physics step is one binary decision (button up / down). A 60-second level at 240 steps/s is 14,400 decisions → a search space of 214400 input sequences. You cannot enumerate that; you can't even enumerate 264. The whole game is finding structure that collapses this space to something a laptop chews through in seconds.
02 The idea

Don't play the game. Play a copy of it — a million times.

The real game runs at 60 frames a second with graphics, sound and menus. Far too slow to try millions of things. So the first step is a simulator — a stripped-down rebuild of Geometry Dash's physics in C++ that does nothing but move the player and check for collisions.

It's headless and blindingly fast: no rendering, just numbers. Feed it a level and a button state and it steps the player forward one frame — where's the icon now, is it dead, did it reach the end? The search calls that little step function hundreds of millions of times, exploring what-ifs no human ever could.

⌗ Under the hood — gd-sim Level sim(levelString) parses the level once. sim.runFrame(pressed, dt) advances the world one physics tick (dt = 1/240) and returns the Player: position, velocity, vehicle, dead, gravity flags. Objects are bucketed by x so collision is local. It reproduces every mechanic — 8 gamemodes, orbs, pads, gravity/size/speed portals, moving triggers, dual mode — closely enough that a solution found in the sim plays back in the real game. Making that "closely enough" true is most of the work (see §6).
03 The core idea — beam search

Instead of one player, imagine 4000 ghosts trying at once.

The naïve approach is one player who commits to each move and hopes. Drive that single guess into a tight ship corridor with the wrong height and it crashes — with no way back. Beam search fixes this by never betting on one guess.

Picture a swarm of ghost-players advancing through the level together, one frame at a time. At each frame every ghost splits in two — one holds the button, one releases — so the swarm fans out to cover every near-future. Ghosts that hit a spike vanish. The rest carry on. Because thousands of futures stay alive simultaneously, the exact mid-air state a brutal jump needs is always still somewhere in the swarm.

A frontier of ghost-trajectories threads a narrowing pinch. Some die at the wall (red); the survivors (mint) carry the swarm through — no single wrong guess can sink the search.

What happens in one frame of the swarm

Expand. Every ghost is simulated one frame forward twice — once holding, once releasing. Dead children are dropped; any child that reaches the finish line ends the search.
Deduplicate. Two ghosts at the same height and speed have identical futures, so only one is kept. On the ground the whole swarm collapses to a handful of states — the search stays cheap until it actually needs to be wide.
Prune smartly. If too many ghosts survive, keep a spread across the full range of positions and speeds — the trick that makes it all work (next section).
Remember the trail. Each ghost stores just a pointer to its parent and its one button-bit. When a ghost finishes, follow the pointers back to the start to reconstruct the exact click sequence.
⌗ Under the hood — width-bounded BFS A breadth-first search over the game-state tree, capped to W = 4000 states per layer. Expansion is state-injection: rollback(0), write the frontier state into gameStates[0], runFrame(). Dedup key = quantised (y·2, velocity/3) plus vehicle/gravity/size/grounded flags — physically-identical states collapse. Reconstruction stores only {parentIndex, pressBit} per layer (a few bytes), so a 20k-frame solve fits in modest memory. No RNG → the same level always solves the same way.
04 The trick that makes it work the crux

Keep a spread of speeds, not just the ghosts in front.

The first version of the swarm kept the ghosts that had travelled furthest. It worked for the wave (which only goes up or down) but went extinct at 50% in ship and UFO sections — and the reason is the whole insight of the project.

In flight modes speed is continuous. Keeping only the fastest ghosts funnels the entire swarm into the same doomed lane, while the one ghost that survives — a hair slower, at just the right height — gets thrown away for "falling behind." The fix: don't rank by progress. Lay a grid over height × speed and keep the best ghost in each cell. Now the swarm always covers the full spread of states, so the precise mid-air speed a corridor demands is guaranteed to still be alive.

↑ height  ·  → speed  — one survivor kept per cell

The frontier is binned into a 2-D grid over (height, velocity) and the best state in each cell is kept. That guarantees the swarm always spans the full phase space — no survivable state gets crowded out by faster-but-doomed neighbours.

On the stress-test level — a 20,000-unit wave / ship / UFO / dual monster:

  • furthest-first pruning → dies at 50%
  • height×speed grid → solves 100%

// keep the frontier diverse: one survivor per (y, velocity) cell
for (state : frontier) {
    yb = bin(state.y,        ymin..ymax, nY);
    vb = bin(state.velocity, vmin..vmax, nV);
    cell = yb*nV + vb;
    if (state.x > cellBest[cell].x)   // furthest-x per cell
        cellBest[cell] = state;
}
frontier = values(cellBest);          // ≤ nY·nV ≈ W, full spread

06 The reality gap

The simulator is 99.99% right. That last 0.01% is the whole game.

A solution is only worth anything if it survives in the real Geometry Dash. So the sim isn't hand-tuned to feel right — it's measured against real recordings of the game, frame by frame. Most of it is astonishingly close.

But "close" isn't "exact," and in a game of frame-perfect spikes, a two-unit disagreement is the difference between a clean clear and instant death. The gap is now hunted, not guessed:

Verified accurate

Per-mode gravity matches the engine's own recorded velocity, and orb/pad impulses match to the exact captured number (pink orb −412.9, blue pad 345.6, …). Cube, ship, ball and wave are pinned down; the rest await recordings.

Caught — a 1-unit hitbox, not a physics bug

Frame-perfect cube + pink-orb levels were unsolvable — the swarm went extinct at ~28%. The trajectory was correct to 0.03u; the culprit was the block-death hitbox being ~1 unit too fat, false-killing a cube grazing a corner. Shrinking that one box (9×9 → 7×7) made those levels solve in ~2 seconds, with zero regressions.

The divergence hunter

A tool replays every recording through the sim, finds the first frame it drifts from reality, and blames it — portal, mini, orb impulse, plain drift, or a false death. Verdict today: no false deaths anywhere, two levels track perfectly to the flag, and the rest drift only 2–3u right at jumps and orbs — the event-timing frontier that's still being closed.

⌗ Under the hood — why the last 2u is so stubborn The residual is a one-frame phase lag: the sim starts a jump's rise a single tick after the real engine, so mid-ascent it sits ~2u low — invisible at the apex, fatal against a floating spike. The obvious fix (move the rise a frame earlier) is entangled with landing + auto-jump timing, so it cascades and breaks other levels. Which is why the last mile isn't more sim-tuning — it's the safety net below.
07 The safety net

The sim proposes. The real engine judges.

Since no from-scratch simulator is ever pixel-exact, the last step doesn't trust it. The solution is played back inside actual Geometry Dash; if it dies, a repair loop nudges the offending clicks — shift a few frames, shorten a hold, drop a press — and re-validates in the real engine until it clears. Because reality is the judge, a repaired solution is guaranteed to work.

The bonus: every fix the real engine demands is a place the sim was wrong, logged with its size. Over many levels that log maps the simulator's blind spots for us — turning "it died somewhere" into "the sim is 3 frames early on this exact jump."

08 Making it human

A solution no human could press is a solution that breaks.

Left unchecked, the search will happily press on every frame — 120 clicks a second, superhuman and frame-perfect. It looks great in the sim and shatters on real playback, where being a fraction of a frame off drifts a ship straight into a spike. Human-limits mode caps the search to ~14 clicks a second and centres each press in the middle of its safe window, so it tolerates real-game jitter.

SUPERHUMAN · frame-perfect · fragile
HUMAN ≤14/s · sparse · robust

Each ghost tracks how long since its last click. A new press is simply forbidden until the cap allows it — and that readiness is folded into the dedup key so the constraint is never pruned away.

On the stress level it still solves 100%, with the click count dropping from ~1886 to 374. If a level genuinely needs faster input, the mode says so instead of emitting an impossible burst.

A full solve, start to finish — steady progress, no wall, no luck. That's the promise of a search that never bets everything on one guess.
